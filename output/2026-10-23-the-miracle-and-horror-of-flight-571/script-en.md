## 2. The Lead Sled’s Error

*[photo: Nando Parrado]*

In October 1972, the Old Christians Club rugby team was at the height of their youth. They chartered a Fairchild FH-227 from the Uruguayan Air Force to fly from Montevideo to Santiago for an international match.

*[broll: vintage turboprop airplane in flight]*

The aircraft was only four years old, but it had already earned a grim nickname among pilots: the 'lead sled.' It was notorious for being underpowered, a dangerous flaw when facing the vertical walls of the Andes.

*[broll: heavy rain night street]*

A massive storm front over the peaks forced an unplanned stop in Mendoza, Argentina. The team spent the night in the city, restless and eager to reach Chile, unaware that the weather was dictating their fate.

*[text: U-SHAPED ROUTE]*

The next day, the pilots chose a U-shaped route to bypass the highest peaks. They planned to fly south to Malargüe before turning west through the Planchón pass, staying well below the aircraft's service ceiling.

*[photo: Uruguayan Air Force Flight 571]*

Pilot Julio César Ferradas was a veteran, but on this leg, he was training co-pilot Dante Héctor Lagurara. Navigation was performed using radio beacons and timing—a process that left zero room for error.

*[broll: flight instruments cockpit navigation]*

At 3:21 p.m., co-pilot Lagurara made a fatal miscalculation. He reported they had reached Curicó and were turning north, but they were actually 60 kilometers east of the turning point, still deep in the mountains.

*[broll: airplane flying into dark thick clouds]*

Unaware of the error, air traffic control cleared the plane to descend. The Fairchild plunged into a thick blanket of clouds, encountering severe turbulence that caused the aircraft to drop hundreds of feet in seconds.

*[photo: Nando Parrado]*

The rugby players joked during the drops, treating the turbulence like a roller coaster. But the laughter died when the plane began to stall and shake, the engines screaming as the pilots tried to climb.

*[quote: the pilots saw the black ridge rising dead ahead.]*

Nando Parrado recalled the moment the clouds finally broke. Instead of the green valleys of Chile, a massive black ridge rose directly in their path, so close they could see the texture of the rock.

*[text: CONTROLLED FLIGHT INTO TERRAIN]*

The ground collision alarm began to scream. The official investigation would call it 'controlled flight into terrain,' but for the survivors, it was the start of a living nightmare.

## 3. Triage at 12,000 Feet

*[photo: Roberto Canessa]*

Inside the smoking fuselage, thirty-three survivors faced a waking nightmare. Near the mangled cockpit where co-pilot Lagurara had been at the controls, medical students Roberto Canessa and Gustavo Zerbino began their triage. They used aircraft insulation to bind limbs and straightened compound fractures with their bare hands.

*[broll: airplane wreckage snow mountain blizzard]*

Outside, a blizzard battered the glacier, threatening to freeze the survivors where they lay. Team captain Marcelo Perez rallied the able-bodied to build a barrier against the freezing wind. They used luggage and broken seats to seal the gaping hole at the back of the cabin.

*[text: -30°C]*

When darkness fell, the temperature plummeted to minus thirty degrees Celsius. The cabin became a freezer for the young men from Montevideo, who were dressed only in light summer blazers. They huddled together in a single mass, fighting to keep their body heat from escaping.

*[photo: Nando Parrado]*

Nando Parrado lay motionless in a deep coma, his skull fractured by the impact. He spent three days on the brink of death, unaware that his mother had already perished in the snow. Just feet away, his sister Susana clung to life as her condition worsened with every hour.

*[broll: vintage transistor radio snow mountain]*

Fito Strauch used sheet metal to melt snow into drinking water, keeping the group alive. They scanned the horizon for rescue planes, but their hope was short-lived. On the eighth day, a transistor radio broadcast the news they feared most: the search had been called off.

## 7. The Impossible Trek

*[photo: Nando Parrado]*

After sixty days of waiting for a rescue that never came, Nando Parrado, Roberto Canessa, and Antonio Vizintín step out onto the glacier. They wear makeshift gear and carry sleeping bags fashioned from plane insulation, embarking on a desperate mission to find Chile.

*[broll: vast snowy mountain range peaks aerial view]*

They scale a towering 4,600-meter peak, expecting to finally look down upon the green valleys of Chile. Instead, their eyes are met by an endless, mocking horizon of jagged, snow-capped mountains stretching as far as the eye can see.

*[text: TEN DAYS ALONE]*

Realizing that three men will deplete their precious meat rations far too quickly, Vizintín turns back to the fuselage. Parrado and Canessa are left alone to continue the march, walking for ten grueling days through the freezing wilderness.

## 8. The Legacy of the Snow

*[broll: helicopter landing in snowy mountains]*

On December 22nd and 23rd, 1972, rescue helicopters finally touched down on the frozen slopes of the glacier. After seventy-two days in the grip of the Andes, sixteen survivors were pulled from the absolute dark. They had endured an ordeal the rest of the world had long since deemed impossible.

*[text: 8 DAYS]*

The group had followed the search for their own lives on a small transistor radio. For eight days, they listened to the search efforts until the broadcast announced the mission was officially over. From that moment, they were dead to the world, left to find their own way out.

*[photo: Nando Parrado]*

Official investigations later scrutinized the final moments before the impact. Records indicate that the co-pilot, Dante Lagurara, was the one at the controls when the plane began its fatal descent into the peaks. The tragedy claimed twenty-nine lives in total, including thirteen who perished in the aftermath and the devastating avalanche.

*[broll: 1970s city street aerial shot]*

When the survivors finally returned home to Montevideo, they faced the immense task of rebuilding their lives. Many went on to become doctors and renowned speakers, carrying the memory of the fallen into their everyday futures.

*[photo: Roberto Canessa]*

Today, the crash site remains a silent, haunting memorial high in the peaks. It is a sacred place where the bond known as the Society of the Snow was forged through unimaginable sacrifice. The sixteen survivors brought back a story that forever redefined the limits of the human spirit.

*[broll: snowy mountain peak wilderness drone shot]*

Their journey stands as a testament to the power of brotherhood and the will to survive. Even in the coldest reach of the Andes, the light of human resilience refused to go out.
